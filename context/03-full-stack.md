# 03 · The Fab City Full Stack Metrics Framework

Source: [B] Vivanco, T., *Fab City Full Stack Metrics Framework: An Actionable Methodology for
Multi-Scalar Implementation* (FAB26 working paper), and [C] Diez, Charny & Kohtala (2024), *The Fab City
Full Stack*.

## The problem it addresses

Fab labs contribute to circularity, education, social inclusion, local economic activity and institutional
experimentation — but their impact is hard to describe with precision. Most evaluation approaches isolate
one dimension, privilege short-term outputs, or fail to connect local interventions to wider urban,
regional and ecological systems.

That difficulty is methodological as much as empirical. The most comprehensive review to date (Peuckert et
al., 2025, ~1,000 studies) found strong quantitative evidence for learning, skills and entrepreneurship
outcomes, and **almost no systematic evidence** for bioregional, place-based or knowledge-sharing layers.

Narrow operational metrics rarely capture governance and social effects. Broad sustainability agendas stay
too distant from practice to guide implementation. What is needed works across scales and dimensions
*without collapsing them into a single index* — hence a matrix rather than a score.

## The architecture

Three axes fold together:

```
        7 LAYERS  (vertical: where change happens)
              ×
        5 SCALES  (horizontal: where it is observed)
              ×
        4 PILLARS (what kind of effect it is)
```

Folding layers against scales and pillars gives the **4 × 5 = 20-cell matrix** the dashboard renders.

## The seven layers

Not a ladder — seven interdependent layers, from the machines in the lab to the knowledge the network
shares back.

| # | Layer | Plain | Functional role |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Infrastructures for production | The machines and the room they sit in | Operational |
| 2 | Capabilities through education | Teaching people to use them | Operational |
| 3 | Value-generating projects | Actually making things that matter to someone | Operational |
| 4 | Orchestrating networks & hubs | Getting labs and groups to work together | Operational |
| 5 | Place-based interventions & policy | Turning it into how the place is actually run | Governance |
| 6 | Bioregional strategies | Fitting it inside what land and water can take | Boundary / horizon |
| 7 | Global knowledge sharing | Sending what you learned back out | Boundary / horizon |

**Layers 1–4 are operational** — closest to direct action and measurable flows.
**Layer 5 is governance-oriented** — it mediates and stabilises the conditions under which impacts can be
monitored and sustained.
**Layers 6–7 are boundary and horizon** — they define the ecological and epistemic reference conditions
against which cumulative impacts must be interpreted.

This grouping clarifies that metrics are not only about outputs. They are also about whether governance
systems can sustain learning, and whether local action aligns with broader limits.

## The five scales

| Scale | Spatial boundary | Time horizon | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Community | Neighbourhood, maker network (~1k–50k people) | Project cycles | Operational — attribution strongest here |
| City | Municipal boundary (~100k–10M) | Annual, 5–10 yr strategy | Governance |
| Region | Multi-city / economic zone (~1M–50M) | 10–20 yr planning | Governance · aggregation ceiling |
| Bioregion | Watershed, ecoregion — ecological not administrative | Ecological cycles | Boundary condition |
| Planet | Global | Generational | Boundary + global knowledge |

This architecture avoids two common errors: assuming local efficiency automatically translates into global
sustainability, and attempting to measure planetary change with indicators only meaningful locally.

The framework is compatible and complementary with Doughnut Economics (Raworth 2017; Bjørn et al. 2023):
local evidence is directly measurable, higher scales serve as interpretive horizons and aggregation
contexts.

## The four pillars

**Environmental** — material flows and circularity, energy and climate, ecosystem impacts, pollution and
toxicity. Most visible at bioregional and planetary scales where pressures accumulate. Material Flow
Analysis and Life Cycle Assessment matter here because without lifecycle thinking, local circularity gains
may simply shift burdens upstream or downstream.

**Social** — health and wellbeing, equity and inclusion, employment and livelihoods, community and
culture, education and capacity. Most directly experienced at community and city scales.

**Economic** — value creation, circular value retention, job creation, income generation, innovation,
resilience. In labs and hubs: whether repair, reuse, local sourcing and entrepreneurial activity actually
retain value locally.

**Governance** — institutional capacity, data availability, participation, transparency, collaboration.

> Governance is not one pillar among others. It is **the condition that enables the other three to become
> durable and legible.** Without regular meetings, clear responsibilities, transparent protocols and
> documented knowledge sharing, the environmental, social and economic metrics cannot be maintained at
> all. Governance is simultaneously a pillar of assessment and a precondition for measurement itself.

## Feasibility tiers

The single most useful filter when choosing what to measure first. Feasibility depends **less on how
sophisticated an indicator is than on how close it sits to paperwork somebody already keeps.**

| Tier | Meaning | Typical source |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | You could pull it from records you already keep, this month | Workshop attendance, repair logs, meeting calendars |
| **Medium** | Exists, but somebody must formalise how it is recorded first | Records needing procedural formalisation |
| **Low** | Needs real instruments or a validated survey. Plan it, don't promise it | Water-reuse monitoring, climate accounting, cohesion indices |

Lower-feasibility indicators should be **deferred to later phases** once core measurement routines are
stable. A compact, well-maintained first-phase indicator set is methodologically stronger than a broad,
weakly-maintained one.

In this dashboard's 51 indicators, the distribution is visible in the **Full Stack** tab.

## The five-stage implementation sequence

From [B] §5.3 — how a lab or hub actually starts.

**Stage 1 — Define boundaries.** Specify the implementation unit, spatial boundary and reporting period.
Usually: the unit is the lab or hub, the boundary is the neighbourhood or user network served, the cycle
is monthly collection with quarterly review. Boundaries are spatial, temporal and functional. Without
this step, numbers risk being misleading or unusable.

**Stage 2 — Map activities.** List recurring activities and map them to layers and pillars. Fabrication
and repair → Layer 1, environmental and economic. Training → Layer 2, social. Partnerships and
coordination → Layer 4, governance.

**Stage 3 — Select indicators.** A first-phase set of **12 to 16 indicators** across all four pillars.
Priority to what can be measured consistently with available records. Balance matters more than quantity.
Ideally selected *with* stakeholders — co-production improves territorial relevance, data availability and
institutional legitimacy.

**Stage 4 — Design data protocols.** Each indicator gets a short protocol sheet: source, unit, method,
collection frequency, responsible person, storage location, known limitations.

**Stage 5 — Review and act.** Monthly for operational tracking, quarterly for strategic learning. Results
adjust programming, partnerships, resource allocation and reporting. Annual review assesses not only
performance but the adequacy of the indicator set itself.

## Four principles for implementation

1. **Begin at community scale**, where interventions are directly experienced and attribution is strongest.
2. **Preserve all four pillars simultaneously**, avoiding the distortions of single-dimension assessment.
3. **Data collection must be transparent, documented and replicable.**
4. **Measurement is a governance and learning instrument**, not retrospective reporting alone.

## Three criteria every indicator must meet

- A clear **unit, source and method**
- Collection **feasible within existing organisational capacity**
- The resulting information must **support an actual decision**

## Planetary computation

The framework's most speculative and most interesting move (Vivanco 2025). Metrics within the Full Stack
do not merely describe performance — when embedded in distributed observation and open-data routines they
enact a form of *regenerative planetary computing*: local data collected in a lab becomes part of a
globally shared intelligence that steers collective action.

Rather than centralising computation in remote platforms, it begins with hyperlocal observation of
ecosystems, communities and material flows, and connects outward through the Full Stack layers,
transforming local evidence into globally readable, collectively governed knowledge.

This reframes measurement not as reporting but as **active participation in a planetary feedback system** —
and it is the conceptual bridge between this framework and the PLANETAI proposition.
